📘 Deep Fundamental Analysis: A Complete Guide
What Is Fundamental Analysis?
Fundamental analysis is the process of determining a stock's intrinsic value — what it is truly worth — by examining the underlying business: its financial statements, competitive position, management quality, and economic environment. The core thesis, championed by Benjamin Graham and Warren Buffett, is simple: price is what you pay, value is what you get. If intrinsic value exceeds market price, the stock is a candidate for purchase; if it falls short, it may be overvalued.
​

🗂️ The Two Pillars
Quantitative Analysis
Hard, measurable numbers from financial statements — ratios, margins, cash flows, growth rates.
​

Qualitative Analysis
Soft factors: business model strength, competitive moat, management integrity, brand value, regulatory environment. Numbers tell you what happened; qualitative tells you why and whether it will continue.
​

🏗️ Phase 1: Understand the Business (Before Any Numbers)
Before opening a spreadsheet, answer these six questions:

What does the company actually do? Can you explain it in two sentences?

Who are its customers and how does it earn revenue? (Recurring SaaS vs. one-time hardware sales are very different)

What is its competitive moat? (Network effects, switching costs, brand, patents, cost advantages)

Who runs it? CEO track record, insider ownership, capital allocation history

What industry/sector is it in? Cyclical (autos, energy) vs. defensive (utilities, healthcare) changes how you interpret every ratio

What are the key risks? Regulatory, competitive, macro, technological disruption

Example: Before analyzing NVDA, you'd note: GPU chip designer, primary customers are data centers and gaming, moat is CUDA software ecosystem (massive switching cost), CEO Jensen Huang owns ~3% of shares ($8B+), in the semiconductor sector (cyclical but driven by AI secular tailwind), key risk is China export restrictions.

Example (NVDA FY2024): Revenue = $60.9B → COGS = $16.6B → Gross Profit = $44.3B (73% gross margin) → Net Income = $29.8B

2.2 Balance Sheet
A snapshot of what the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) at a point in time.

text
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
Key items: Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, PP&E (assets) vs. Accounts Payable, Long-term Debt (liabilities), Retained Earnings (equity).

2.3 Cash Flow Statement
Tracks actual cash movement — the most manipulation-resistant statement. Three sections:

Section	What It Shows	Why It Matters
Operating Cash Flow (OCF)	Cash generated from core business	Reveals true earnings quality
Investing Cash Flow	CapEx, acquisitions, asset sales	Shows growth investment vs. asset disposal
Financing Cash Flow	Debt issuance/repayment, dividends, buybacks	Capital structure decisions
Red Flag Example: A company shows Net Income of $500M but OCF of only $50M. This gap suggests aggressive revenue recognition or poor receivables collection — earnings are not converting to real cash.

📊 Phase 3: Valuation Ratios
3.1 Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio
Formula: 
P/E
=

Earnings Per Share (EPS)
P/E=

What it means: How much investors pay per $1 of earnings. A P/E of 25 means you're paying $25 for every $1 of annual profit.
​

Two variants:

Trailing P/E — uses last 12 months actual EPS (backward-looking, factual)

Forward P/E — uses next 12 months estimated EPS (forward-looking, subject to analyst error)

Benchmarks: S&P 500 long-run average ~16x; current ~22x. Always compare to sector peers, not the whole market.
​

Example: AAPL trades at P/E 28x vs. consumer electronics sector average 20x → premium of 40%. Justified if Apple grows faster than peers; unjustified if growth is slowing.

Pitfall: P/E is meaningless for companies with negative earnings (early-stage, cyclical trough years). Use EV/EBITDA instead.

What it means: Adjusts P/E for growth rate. A stock growing at 30%/year deserves a higher P/E than one growing at 5%. Peter Lynch popularized the rule: PEG < 1.0 = potentially undervalued.
​

Example: Stock A: P/E = 30, EPS growth = 30% → PEG = 1.0 (fairly valued)
Stock B: P/E = 30, EPS growth = 10% → PEG = 3.0 (expensive for its growth)

Pitfall: Growth estimates can be wildly wrong. Use 3–5 year consensus growth, not a single year.

What it means: How much you pay vs. the net assets the company owns. P/B < 1.0 means the market values the company below its liquidation value — either a deep value opportunity or a value trap.
​

Best used for: Banks, insurance companies, real estate — asset-heavy businesses where book value is meaningful.
Less useful for: Tech companies where most value is intangible (brand, IP, software).

Example: JPMorgan P/B ~1.7x (reasonable for banks). Meta P/B ~8x (intangible-heavy; book value understates true worth).

What it means: Useful for unprofitable or early-stage companies where P/E doesn't work. Lower is generally better. A P/S above 10x requires exceptional growth justification.
​

Example: A SaaS startup with $100M revenue trading at $1B market cap = P/S of 10x. If growing 50%/year, this could be reasonable. If growing 10%/year, it's expensive.

Where Enterprise Value (EV) = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash

What it means: The most widely used valuation multiple by M&A professionals and hedge funds. It strips out capital structure (debt/equity mix) and accounting differences (depreciation), enabling apples-to-apples comparison across companies.
​

Benchmarks: S&P 500 median ~14x; Tech ~20–25x; Utilities ~10–12x

Example: Company A (no debt, $100M EBITDA, $1.4B market cap) EV/EBITDA = 14x.
Company B (same EBITDA, but $500M in debt, $1.4B market cap) EV/EBITDA = 19x — the debt makes it more expensive on enterprise terms even though the stock price looks the same.

Formula:

What it means: Many analysts consider FCF the purest measure of a company's earnings power because it's hard to fake with accounting. A P/FCF below the P/E suggests strong earnings quality.

Example: If a stock has P/E = 25 but P/FCF = 18, it means the company converts earnings to cash efficiently — a positive quality signal. If P/FCF > P/E significantly, earnings quality is suspect.

4.4 Return on Equity (ROE)
Formula: 
ROE
=

ROE=

What it means: How efficiently management uses shareholder capital to generate profit. Buffett's threshold: consistently above 15% indicates a durable competitive advantage. Apple and Microsoft regularly exceed 30%.
​

Important caveat: A company can inflate ROE by taking on excessive debt (reduces equity denominator). Always check ROE alongside the debt/equity ratio.

Example: A company with $100M net income and $500M equity = ROE of 20%. If they buy back $200M of stock (equity drops to $300M) and still earn $100M, ROE jumps to 33% — without any improvement in underlying business.

Formula:

Where NOPAT = Net Operating Profit After Tax; Invested Capital = Equity + Debt − Cash

What it means: The gold standard profitability metric. Unlike ROE, it cannot be gamed by leverage. Companies that consistently earn ROIC > their cost of capital (WACC) are creating shareholder value; those below are destroying it.
​

Example: ROIC = 25%, WACC = 10% → the company earns 15 cents of excess return for every dollar invested. Over time, this compounding is why quality-compounder stocks outperform.

What it means: How efficiently the company deploys its total asset base. More useful for asset-heavy businesses (manufacturing, retail). ROA > 5% is generally considered good; asset-light businesses (software) can exceed 20%.

5.2 EPS Growth
More important than revenue growth for shareholders because it measures per-share earnings power. Watch for the gap between revenue and EPS growth — if EPS grows much faster than revenue, the company is either expanding margins or buying back shares. Both are positive, but for different reasons.

Example: A company grows revenue 10% but EPS 25%. Investigation reveals margin expansion from 15% → 20% net margin AND a 5% share count reduction from buybacks. This is quality earnings growth.

5.3 Free Cash Flow Growth
FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures

The most reliable growth metric. Revenue and earnings can be manipulated; FCF is much harder to fake. A company consistently growing FCF at 15%+ is among the most powerful compounding machines in investing.

💸 Phase 8: Dividend Analysis
Relevant for income stocks, dividend ETFs, and retirement portfolios.

Metric	Formula	Healthy Benchmark
Dividend Yield	Annual Dividend / Stock Price	2–5% (above 6% may signal distress)
Payout Ratio	Dividends Paid / Net Income	< 60% (sustainable); > 80% = risk
Dividend Coverage Ratio	EPS / DPS	> 2x (comfortable buffer)
FCF Payout Ratio	Dividends Paid / FCF	< 70% (more conservative than EPS-based)
Dividend CAGR (5Y)	Growth rate of dividend over 5 years	> 5% signals commitment to income growth
Red Flag Example: A utility company with 95% payout ratio and declining revenue. Any earnings shock forces a dividend cut — and dividend cut stocks historically drop 20–30% immediately.

📐 Phase 9: Earnings Quality Assessment
High earnings quality means reported profits closely mirror real cash generation. Check these:

Signal	Good Quality	Poor Quality
OCF vs Net Income	OCF > Net Income	OCF significantly < Net Income
Accruals Ratio	Near zero	Large positive accruals (earnings not backed by cash)
Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)	Stable or declining	Rising DSO (customers taking longer to pay)
Revenue Recognition	Conservative, milestone-based	Aggressive, front-loaded
Non-GAAP adjustments	Minor, one-time	Recurring "one-time" items every quarter
Example: A company reports $1B net income but $300M OCF. The $700M gap consists of $400M in accounts receivable growth (revenue booked but not collected) and $300M in inventory build. This warrants scrutiny — is the revenue real or are products piling up unsold?

⚠️ Common Pitfalls to Avoid
Recency bias: Don't extrapolate 3 years of bull-market growth into a DCF forever

Ignoring sector context: A P/E of 30 is expensive for utilities but cheap for hypergrowth SaaS

Overweighting single metrics: No one ratio tells the whole story; always triangulate

Ignoring qualitative factors: A perfectly valued mediocre business beats a cheaply priced terrible one

Anchoring to purchase price: Fundamental analysis should be forward-looking; past losses are sunk costs
